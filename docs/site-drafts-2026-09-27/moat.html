The Only Moat Is the Record

Everything in this estate is free to copy, adapt and build on. That is the design, not the risk. What a copy cannot carry away is the record it came from.

Why it stays open

The estate’s own argument settles this. The Tenant’s Record names three moats that kept care organisations renting their systems — build cost, operations, and governance — and says of the third: “The governance moat is the reason this estate exists.” The instruments a care organisation needs to govern its own system are “written, published, and open access … Free to take, adapt, cite, and deploy against.”

An estate that took one moat down by publishing cannot build a moat of its own by withholding. So every record is open access under CC BY 4.0, and the Glass Ledger’s code is MIT. There is no fee and no registration wall.

CC BY 4.0 lets anyone copy, adapt and use the work, commercially included. Anyone who passes it on, adapted or not, must credit the author, point to the licence (and to the source where reasonably practicable), and say whether it was changed. That is all the protection the estate has, and it covers what matters.

What a copy cannot take

A worksheet can be lifted in a minute. None of the following can, and you can check each one from outside without asking anyone.

  • Date
    Every record carries a DOI and a deposit timestamp recorded by Zenodo, not by this site or its author. The trilogy — The 24-Hour Team, The Mixed Shift and The Long Handover — was deposited on 7 July 2026. The latest records were deposited on 27 September 2026. Revisions go up as new versions under one concept DOI; the Reading Map’s three versions are all still readable. Predictions are dated too: After the Machine registers its three as “dated at publication — 23 July 2026”.
  • Checksum
    Zenodo publishes an md5 checksum for every file. The Instrument Deck’s sources were “md5-verified against Zenodo’s own recorded checksums before extraction”. Each of its sections opens with a divider naming the parent document and its DOI, and each facsimile page carries its source’s title and author — in the Deck’s words, “a photocopy of this deck still cites itself.” Anyone handed a file can run the same check against the record.
  • Author
    Twenty-two records, one creator on each: Blatherwick, Paul. The trilogy’s covers state the author’s standing: “Registered Mental Health Nurse · Sixteen years in clinical practice”. His NMC PIN is on the founder page, so you can check it against the register. Where a model helped with drafting, papers such as The Tenant’s Record name it and state that the claims, citations and judgements are the author’s. A copy can reproduce the words. It cannot change who answers for them.
  • Interlock
    The Reading Map indexes twenty-one documents and sends fourteen kinds of reader to a starting point. It sets an order of operations: build the team, clear the gate, hire the mind, seat the overseers, measure the floor — and for the person in care, assess, measure, record. The Instrument Deck binds eight instruments in the order a deployment actually meets them, each section under its parent DOI. The parts are built to agree. The Damping Audit escalates by the “same rule, same wording, as the Risk Screen — deliberately, so a service running both instruments runs one discipline”. The Floor Test is written as the validation instrument for six named papers. A single worksheet can be lifted; the cross-referenced system it belongs to cannot be, without re-deriving it.
  • Working
    Parts of the estate run as code. The Glass Ledger is roughly six hundred lines of MIT-licensed Python, and its record can be verified by anyone with the file and a public key. Learning Outside the Model turned fifty-one safety claims into tests. Seven failed on first execution, and the paper reports all seven alongside the changes that fixed them.
  • Correctable
    The estate is written so that it can be caught out. Predictions are registered in advance: four in The Cultural Safety Case, four in The Second Pair of Hands and three in After the Machine. Many of the papers declare the evidence tier behind their claims; the Damping Audit calls itself “Tier C — internally coherent, unvalidated”. The Risk Screen was revised in the open after named clinical review. The Reading Map commits the series to versioned revision, with contradicting results “published with thanks”. The Floor Test puts the same rule more bluntly: “If a claim dies on enough floors, it dies in print, with thanks.”

Why attribution is enough

CC BY does not stop anyone copying, and it is not meant to. For a governance estate it does something more useful. Every copy that is passed on must carry the author’s name and say whether it was changed. A service that publishes an adapted Risk Screen has to say that it adapted it, and whose it was. Its version then sits beside a dated, checksummed original that anyone can compare it with. Copies spread the record. They do not replace it.

What this page does not claim

  • It does not claim that nobody else could write governance instruments for AI in care. The claim is narrower: this record, with these dates, this author and these parts that fit together, can be checked, and its dates were recorded by Zenodo rather than claimed by the author.
  • It does not claim the instruments are validated. Several say plainly that they are not, and say what evidence would change that.
  • It does not claim that owning a system is free. The Tenant’s Record says the operations moat still stands: “any paper telling you ownership is free is selling you something.”
